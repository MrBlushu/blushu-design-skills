# Experiments and prototypes

Use this reference to select the smallest credible learning activity and to interpret its result without overstating certainty.

## Start with the decision, not the method

Before proposing research or a prototype, state:

1. the decision that may change;
2. the assumption or risk blocking it;
3. the observable signal that could discriminate among actions;
4. the cost of being wrong;
5. the time and exposure available.

If no realistic result would change the decision, do not run the activity. It is demonstration, alignment, or delivery work rather than a discovery test.

## Choose a proportional method

| Learning need | Prefer | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand situation, workaround, or problem history | Interviews grounded in recent concrete behavior, observation, support or operational records | Hypothetical preference and leading questions |
| Test interest or commitment | Concierge/manual service, pre-commitment, bounded fake door, landing proposition, or realistic choice | Clicks or compliments that carry no meaningful cost |
| Test comprehension and task flow | Storyboard or interactive prototype at the minimum necessary fidelity | Visual polish masking a weak task or value proposition |
| Test technical possibility | Spike, simulation, benchmark, or thin integration | Demo conditions unlike production constraints |
| Test operational or channel fit | Service rehearsal, workflow simulation, pilot, or partner review with real constraints | Approval opinions detached from actual operations |
| Test sustained value | Limited live pilot with usage and outcome observation | Novelty effects and self-selected early enthusiasts |

Use a dedicated usability-review skill when the task is to diagnose interface friction in detail. Product discovery owns the risk and decision, not the specialist review procedure.

## Write a test card

Define before execution:

- **Decision:** action this result will influence.
- **Target risk:** one primary assumption or a tightly coupled set.
- **Participants/data:** target, recruitment or source, context, exclusions.
- **Artifact/environment:** minimum fidelity and realism needed.
- **Task/exposure:** what participants encounter or data is evaluated.
- **Observable signals:** behaviors, outcomes, failures, costs, or constraints.
- **Supports:** result that makes the assumption more credible.
- **Weakens:** result that makes the assumption less credible.
- **Indeterminate:** result that cannot discriminate, including why.
- **Safeguards:** consent, privacy, deception limits, accessibility, safety, and rollback.
- **Next action:** what happens under each result class.

Avoid a pass/fail threshold chosen only after seeing results.

## Set fidelity by uncertainty

Use the least fidelity that preserves the behavior under investigation:

- use words or a storyboard for concept and sequence;
- use an interactive prototype for navigation, comprehension, or task behavior;
- use a concierge or Wizard-of-Oz service for value before automation;
- use a technical spike for capability, performance, or integration;
- use a limited live implementation only when real environment or repeated behavior is essential.

Higher fidelity is not stronger evidence by default. It can increase cost, attachment, participant expectations, and exposure without improving the inference.

## Protect users and trust

For fake doors, simulations, or incomplete services:

1. bound who can be exposed and for how long;
2. avoid collecting data that is unnecessary for the learning goal;
3. prevent financial, safety, privacy, or reputational harm;
4. provide disclosure or debrief when deception could affect trust;
5. define rollback and support before launch;
6. refuse the method when the learning value does not justify the exposure.

## Interpret results

After the activity:

1. Record observations before explanations.
2. Compare results with the predeclared supports, weakens, and indeterminate signals.
3. Identify recruitment, environment, novelty, facilitator, instrumentation, and survivorship effects.
4. Separate evidence about problem/value, use/comprehension, feasibility, and context.
5. State what the activity did not test.
6. Update the claim ledger and risk queue.
7. Choose `proceed`, `continue discovery`, `reframe/pause`, or `stop` for the affected decision.

Do not claim that one favorable activity validates a product. Report the uncertainty reduced, the uncertainty retained, and the commitment now justified.

## Iterate deliberately

Update an artifact only when the observed problem is relevant to the target risk. If the result exposes a different, more consequential assumption, reprioritize before polishing the current prototype.
