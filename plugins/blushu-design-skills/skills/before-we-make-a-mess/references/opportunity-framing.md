# Opportunity framing

Use this reference to turn a feature request, idea, or broad opportunity into a decision that discovery can resolve.

## Establish the decision

Write one sentence:

> Decide whether to [commitment] for [target] in order to [outcome] before [decision point].

Name the current mode:

- **Opportunity:** decide whether the problem merits discovery.
- **Candidate solution:** decide whether this direction merits validation.
- **Readiness:** decide whether evidence is sufficient for the next commitment.

If the request is already a delivery, interaction-pattern, usability-review, or copy task with no unresolved product decision, route it instead of manufacturing discovery work.

## Build the opportunity frame

Capture only what can change the decision:

| Field | Required question | Adequacy check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target | Who experiences the problem, buys, approves, or supports the solution? | Separate materially different roles rather than collapsing them into one persona. |
| Situation | In what context does the problem occur? | Describe a concrete trigger, task, or constraint. |
| Problem | What prevents progress or creates loss today? | State the observed difficulty, not the proposed feature. |
| Current alternative | What does the target do now, including manual, offline, indirect, or no-action options? | Include the status quo and switching cost. |
| Desired outcome | What change should the target or product achieve? | Make it observable without embedding the solution. |
| Success signal | What would show movement toward the outcome? | Prefer behavior or operational change over artifact completion. |
| Why now | What changed, or what decision deadline exists? | Distinguish urgency evidence from stakeholder enthusiasm. |
| Advantage | Why might this organization solve it credibly? | Name capabilities or access, not generic ambition. |
| Constraints | What limits the solution or the investigation? | Include technology, channel, policy, economics, safety, accessibility, and time when material. |
| Recommendation needed | What action can the decision maker take next? | Make the available commitments explicit. |

Mark every unsupported field as an assumption or unknown. Do not turn a plausible answer into a fact.

## Reframe a feature request

1. Preserve the requested feature as a **candidate solution**, not a requirement.
2. Ask what user or business change the requester expects from it.
3. Identify who experiences that change and in what situation.
4. Find the current workaround or alternative.
5. State the most consequential assumption linking the feature to the outcome.
6. Rewrite the decision without the feature name.
7. Return to the feature only after the problem frame can support or reject it.

Example transformation:

- Request: “Add AI summaries to every project.”
- Candidate assumption: “People miss important changes because reviewing updates takes too long.”
- Decision: “Decide whether reducing update-review time for active project leads is important enough to explore an automated summary.”

## Handle multiple stakeholders

Separate at least these roles when they differ:

- user who performs the task;
- customer or buyer who pays;
- approver who accepts risk;
- operator or support role who absorbs downstream cost;
- channel partner who influences adoption.

Do not optimize for all roles equally. Name the primary target for the current decision and record conflicts as risks or constraints.

## Prioritize product principles

Convert generic principles into an ordered trade-off:

1. List only principles that could change the solution.
2. Create a plausible conflict between the top two.
3. State which principle wins for this target and situation.
4. Record the cost of that priority and when it should be revisited.

“Simple, secure, and fast” is not a decision until the team can resolve a conflict among them.

## Determine framing readiness

The opportunity is framed well enough when:

- the decision and decision point are explicit;
- target, situation, problem, alternative, and outcome are distinguishable;
- known constraints and stakeholders are visible;
- unsupported claims are marked rather than silently completed;
- at least one risk can be tested or consciously accepted.

Do not block on polish. If missing information does not change the next learning action, proceed conditionally and keep the gap visible.
