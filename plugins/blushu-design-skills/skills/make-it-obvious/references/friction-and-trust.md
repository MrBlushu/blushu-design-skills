# Friction and trust

Use this reference for multi-step tasks, forms, consequential choices, errors, mobile interfaces, waits, transparency, and recovery. Evaluate the work transferred to the person, not simply the number of screens or controls.

## Trace the task cost

Walk through the task and record each demand:

- interpret a label or category;
- locate an action or required fact;
- remember information across states;
- enter, transform, or repeat data;
- wait without useful progress feedback;
- decide under uncertainty;
- recover from an error or wrong path;
- leave the product to obtain information or complete a step.

Mark a demand as avoidable when the interface already has the information, can safely derive it, can defer it, or can make the choice self-explanatory. Preserve effort that is necessary for safety, informed consent, learning, or the actual value-producing task.

## Judge choices by uncertainty, not click count

- Prefer a sequence of clear, confident decisions over one overloaded decision.
- Keep the likely destination or outcome of each choice evident.
- Reduce repeated traversal when the same path is frequent or each transition is slow.
- Expose consequences before irreversible or costly actions.
- Do not collapse steps when doing so combines unrelated decisions or hides confirmation.

More steps are acceptable while the person remains confident and each step advances the task. Fewer steps are not an improvement if they increase ambiguity or error risk.

## Place assistance at the decision

When guidance is necessary, make it:

- **brief:** only the fact needed for this decision;
- **timely:** encountered at the moment of uncertainty;
- **noticeable:** positioned and styled so the relevant person is likely to perceive it.

Prefer clear labels, examples, defaults, constraints, and inline validation over a block of instructions before the task. Keep help recoverable for unfamiliar or infrequent interactions; do not show onboarding once and make it impossible to revisit.

## Review forms and errors

- Ask only for information required now; defer optional or later-use fields.
- Use labels and answer formats that match the person's knowledge, not internal data models.
- Explain why sensitive or unexpected information is required.
- Preserve valid input after an error.
- Associate error messages with the affected field and state the repair action.
- Prevent predictable errors when prevention does not remove necessary control.
- Provide cancellation, undo, correction, or a safe return path proportional to consequence.

Treat a confusing error as more severe when the person cannot diagnose whether data was saved, payment occurred, or the task can continue.

## Review mobile constraints

- Prioritize frequent and urgent actions, but retain an evident path to less common capabilities.
- Do not assume mobile means “on the move” or justify an incomplete product without evidence.
- Ensure touch actions do not depend on hover or invisible gestures.
- Make controls perceivable and comfortably operable without stripping useful distinctions for visual purity.
- Preserve the requested destination when a person arrives from email, search, notification, or another app.
- Account for intermittent networks and interruptions; preserve state and explain delays.
- Consider learnability and relearning when essential actions use nonstandard gestures or hidden modes.

Do not copy device-specific prescriptions from old examples. Apply the durable constraint: limited space and unreliable context increase the need for priority, visible affordances, continuity, and recovery.

## Review waits and perceived performance

Treat delay as a usability issue when it changes behavior or confidence.

- Show that an action was received.
- Distinguish progress, background work, stalled state, and failure.
- Display useful content before secondary media or promotions when possible.
- Avoid making the person repeat an action because feedback is absent.
- Preserve control during long operations when cancellation is safe.

Route bundle analysis, network profiling, and implementation optimization to performance engineering. This skill records the task consequence and required experience.

## Review transparency and goodwill

Look for behavior that spends trust unnecessarily:

- hiding costs, constraints, outages, eligibility, or material consequences;
- making support or cancellation harder than purchase;
- asking the person to repeat information the product already holds;
- presenting promotions while a blocking problem is unresolved;
- disguising marketing as help or frequently asked questions;
- failing to acknowledge a known inconvenience.

Recommend candor, direct access to primary tasks, useful current answers, automated handoffs, and explicit recovery. Do not frame legitimate business constraints as defects; expose the tradeoff and make the user cost deliberate.

## Validate the improvement

Choose an observable check tied to the original cost:

- first-choice confidence for ambiguous options;
- completion and recovery behavior for flows;
- error recurrence and successful repair for forms;
- repeated-action count for redundant work;
- abandonment or support contacts when reliable metrics exist;
- mobile task completion across interruption or weak-network states.

Avoid claiming that trust increased without a proxy. State the reduced surprise, repetition, uncertainty, or recovery cost instead.
